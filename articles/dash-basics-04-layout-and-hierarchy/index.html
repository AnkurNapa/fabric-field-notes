Layout and visual hierarchy

Readers scan a page top left first, so the most important number goes there and the detail goes down and to the right. Lay out the Harbour Goods page on a grid, size things by importance and keep the slicers in one place.

11 Oct 2026 Fabric as of October 2026 Checked against Microsoft Learn Ankur Napa Power BI

Short answer

Visual hierarchy means the most important thing on the page looks the most important: bigger, higher and further left. Readers of left-to-right languages scan from the top left, so put the headline numbers there, the trend and breakdown below and the detail at the bottom. Align everything to a grid, keep the page to one screen without scrolling and give each page one job.

Why it matters for your career

Two reports with identical numbers can get opposite reactions. The one where the reader finds the answer in three seconds gets used; the other gets an email asking for "a simpler version". Layout is the cheapest quality upgrade you can make. It is also the easiest to show in a portfolio.

The idea

Think of the page as three bands, from summary to detail:

 +--------------------------------------------------------------+
 | Harbour Goods sales, Jul to Sep 2026        [Month] [Region] |  title and slicers
 +--------------+--------------+--------------+-----------------+
 | Net sales    | Margin %     | Orders       | Avg order value |  band 1: the answer
 +--------------+--------------+--------------+-----------------+
 | Net sales by month (columns)       | Net sales by city (bars) |  band 2: where and when
 +------------------------------------+--------------------------+
 | Margin % by category and month (matrix)                       |  band 3: detail
 +---------------------------------------------------------------+

Rules that make this work:

RuleWhyOn this page
Biggest thing is the most importantEqual sizes give the eye nowhere to startCards span the full width
Top left firstThat is where the scan beginsNet sales card sits top left
Summary above detailReaders drill with their eyesMatrix at the bottom
Filters in one consistent placePeople should not hunt for themSlicers top right, same spot on every page
One page, one jobA page that answers everything answers nothingProduct detail moves to its own page
White space between groupsSpacing shows what belongs togetherA gap between bands, not between cards

Two words worth knowing. Alignment means edges line up, so the page reads as organised. Grouping means related visuals sit close together and unrelated ones further apart, so spacing does the work a border would otherwise do.

Try it in Fabric

Open rpt_harbour_sales in Edit mode with the visuals from lesson 3.

  1. Turn on the grid. On the View menu turn on gridlines and snap to grid (the labels may read Show gridlines and Snap to grid). Visuals now snap to the nearest grid line when you move or resize them. Smart guides also appear as you drag, to line a visual up with its neighbour.
  2. Title. Insert a text box across the top: "Harbour Goods sales, Jul to Sep 2026". Make it the largest text on the page.
  3. Band 1. Stretch the card visual across the full width under the title. If you made separate cards, give them identical heights and the same gap between each.
  4. Band 2. Put the column chart on the left two thirds and the city bar chart on the right third. Match their tops and heights exactly.
  5. Band 3. Put the matrix across the bottom.
  6. Reading order. Open the Selection pane from the View menu. Its order sets the layer order; its Tab order view sets the order a keyboard user moves through the visuals. Arrange it title, cards, column chart, bar chart, matrix.
  7. Save.

Check it worked: stand back from the screen (or zoom the browser out to 50 per cent) and ask a colleague which number matters most. If they point at net sales top left, the hierarchy works. If no scroll bar appears on the page at your normal zoom, it fits on one screen.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Everything the same size. Twelve equal tiles are a grid of shrugs. Make the headline numbers bigger.
  • Decoration as hierarchy. Shadows, borders and background images on every visual add noise. Use space and size first.
  • Scroll bars. If the page needs scrolling, it has two jobs. Split it.
  • Slicers moving between pages. Put them in the same place on every page, at the same size, with the same fonts. Microsoft's accessibility checklist asks for this too.
  • Ignoring where it is viewed. A wall screen can hold more; a phone holds far less. Ask before you design. Use the mobile layout for phone users.

On the brewhouse floor

At Distillery A the still house screen used to show 14 equal tiles, from steam pressure to the canteen headcount. The stillman needs one thing first: is the spirit run on the right cut? Put the current strength coming off the spirit still top left in the largest type, the run's strength trend beside it and everything else smaller below. In a still house the order of the page is the order of the decisions.
