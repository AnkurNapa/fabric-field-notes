Data analysis basics, lesson 2 of 8

Asking a good question

Lesson 2 of Data analysis basics. A good analysis question names the measure, the time window, the grouping and the decision it feeds. You will see two honest answers to the same vague question disagree on the Harbour Goods data.

11 Oct 2026 Fabric as of October 2026 Checked against Microsoft Learn Ankur Napa Lakehouse

Short answer

A good question tells you exactly what to count, over what period, split by what and why someone wants to know. "How are sales?" is not a question you can query. "What was net revenue per store in September 2026, so we can decide where to send the new stock?" is. Most wrong reports are not caused by bad SQL. They are caused by answering a different question from the one that was asked.

Why it matters for your career

Managers remember the analyst who asked one clarifying question and came back with the right answer, not the one who came back fast with the wrong one. Shaping questions is the skill that separates a junior analyst from a trusted one. It costs nothing to learn.

The idea

Every sharp question has four parts. Write them down before you write any SQL.

PartPlain meaningHarbour Goods example
Measurethe number you will calculatenet revenue in INR, after discount
Windowthe time period1 to 30 September 2026
Groupinghow you split itper store
Decisionwhat the answer changeswhere to send next month's stock

Some words to pin down. A measure is a number you add up or average, such as revenue or units. A dimension is something you group by, such as store or month. Net revenue here means quantity times unit price, minus the discount. Discount is stored as a fraction, so 0.05 means 5 per cent off.

Watch how much the measure matters. "Which store did best in September?" can mean most order lines or most money. On this data the two answers disagree.

Try it in Fabric

Use the lh_harbour lakehouse and orders table from lesson 1.

  1. Open lh_harbour. In the top right of the ribbon, switch the dropdown from Lakehouse to SQL analytics endpoint.
  2. Select New SQL query.
  3. Paste this and select Run. Do not worry about the grammar yet; lessons 3 and 4 explain every line.
SELECT
    store_id,
    COUNT(*) AS order_lines,
    CAST(SUM(quantity * unit_price * (1 - discount)) AS decimal(12,2)) AS revenue_inr
FROM dbo.orders
WHERE order_date >= '2026-09-01' AND order_date < '2026-10-01'
GROUP BY store_id
ORDER BY revenue_inr DESC;

Read it twice. By order lines ST-022 ties for first. By revenue it is fifth. ST-011 took only two orders but one of them was four Bluetooth speakers, so it comes second. Neither answer is wrong. They answer different questions, which is why you agree the measure before you start.

There is also a store called ST-099. Harbour Goods has six stores and none of them is ST-099. Keep that in mind. You will chase it down in lesson 5. One of ST-014's September lines also has a date that does not exist, which lesson 6 deals with.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Not asking "so that what?" If you know the decision, you know what level of detail matters. Stock decisions need units; a bonus scheme needs revenue.
  • Leaving the window open. "Last month" changes meaning on the first of every month. Write the dates.
  • Mixing gross and net. Revenue before discount and after discount are both "revenue" in conversation. Say which one in the column name.
  • Answering silently. When the data has a problem, like a store that does not exist, say so in the answer instead of quietly dropping it.

On the brewhouse floor

A winery owner asks "how was the harvest?" The analyst turns it into "tonnes of fruit received per block, per variety, between 1 and 20 March, so we can plan tank space." Count by truck loads and the busiest block looks best. Count by tonnes and a block that sent three heavy loads wins. Same table of weighbridge tickets, two honest answers, so agree the measure first.
