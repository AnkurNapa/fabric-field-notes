Data analysis basics, lesson 8 of 8

From query to answer: explore in a notebook, then write the finding

Lesson 8 of Data analysis basics. A query gives you numbers; an answer tells someone what happened, how you know and what to be careful of. Explore the clean Harbour Goods orders in a Fabric notebook, chart the quarter and write a three-sentence finding a manager can act on.

11 Oct 2026 Fabric as of October 2026 Checked against Microsoft Learn Ankur Napa LakehouseSpark

Short answer

The job is not finished when the query runs. It is finished when the person who asked understands the answer and knows how far to trust it. Explore the data until you can explain the number, then write it down in three sentences: what happened, the evidence and the caveat. A Fabric notebook is a good place to explore because you can mix queries, charts and notes on one page.

Why it matters for your career

Managers rarely read queries. They read the two lines at the top of your email. Analysts who write a clear finding with an honest caveat get asked the next question; the ones who send a table and a chart get asked what it means.

The idea

Back in lesson 2 a question had four parts: measure, window, grouping and decision. A finding mirrors it in three sentences.

PartWhat it saysHarbour Goods example
Answerwhat happened, in plain wordsnet revenue fell every month of the quarter
Evidencethe numbers that show it and whyfewer order lines in August, smaller lines in September
Caveatwhat you removed, assumed or cannot knowfour faulty rows left out, one unknown store, only three months

Exploring is how you get the middle sentence. A total that fell can fall because there were fewer sales or because each sale was smaller. Those lead to different decisions, so split the number until you know which.

Try it in Fabric

  1. Open lh_harbour from lesson 1 and select Open notebook > New notebook. It opens with lh_harbour attached. Rename it nb_harbour_explore.
  2. Paste each block into its own cell and run them in order. %%sql at the top tells the cell to run Spark SQL, the notebook's own SQL dialect.

The orders_clean view from lesson 6 lives in the SQL analytics endpoint, which the notebook's Spark engine cannot see. So the first cell writes the same three rules again as a temporary view that lasts for this session:

%%sql
CREATE OR REPLACE TEMPORARY VIEW orders_clean AS
SELECT DISTINCT
    order_id,
    try_cast(order_date AS date) AS order_date,
    store_id,
    product_id,
    quantity,
    round(quantity * unit_price * (1 - discount), 2) AS revenue_inr
FROM dbo.orders
WHERE quantity > 0
  AND try_cast(order_date AS date) IS NOT NULL;

Now split monthly revenue into its two parts, how many lines and how big each line was:

%%sql
SELECT
    date_format(order_date, 'yyyy-MM') AS order_month,
    COUNT(*)                     AS order_lines,
    round(SUM(revenue_inr), 2)   AS revenue_inr,
    round(AVG(revenue_inr), 2)   AS mean_line
FROM orders_clean
GROUP BY date_format(order_date, 'yyyy-MM')
ORDER BY order_month;
  1. Under the result, switch from the table to the chart view. The label may read Chart or New chart. Pick a column chart with order_month on the axis and revenue_inr as the value.
  2. Add a markdown cell (the label may read + Markdown or Text) under the chart and write your three sentences there, so the reasoning sits next to the evidence.

Check it worked. The first cell runs without output. The second returns:

The total matches lesson 6: 153,919.85 INR over 56 lines. From July to August the line count fell by almost a quarter, which explains most of the drop. From August to September the count held at 17 and the average line shrank instead. Your finding could read:

Harbour Goods' net revenue fell in each month of the quarter, from 68,134.50 INR in July to 40,725.75 INR in September. July to August lost five order lines (22 down to 17); August to September kept 17 lines but each was worth less on average (2,650.56 down to 2,395.63 INR). These figures leave out four faulty till rows worth 2,350 INR net and include one 854.05 INR sale from ST-099, a store missing from the store list; three months is too short to call a trend.

If the cell fails on dbo.orders, check the notebook's lakehouse in the Explorer on the left. If it fails on a cast, check Workspace settings > Spark: Runtime 2.0 became the default in October 2026 and is strict about conversions, which is why the code uses try_cast.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Sending the chart without the sentence. A chart shows what; only you can say why.
  • Leaving out the caveat. If the manager finds the missing rows later, every past number you sent becomes doubtful.
  • Exploring for ever. Stop when you can explain the number and name what would change your mind.
  • Rules in two places drifting apart. The notebook repeats lesson 6's filter. In a real team the data engineer publishes one clean table both can use; see Bronze, silver and gold, simply.

On the brewhouse floor

The owner of Distillery A asks why spirit output fell this month. The analyst splits litres of pure alcohol, bulk litres times strength, into two parts: how many runs the still made and how much each run yielded. Runs held steady but yield per run dropped, which points at the still, not the rota. The finding names the two weeks, gives both numbers and adds the caveat that one run's strength reading was never logged.
