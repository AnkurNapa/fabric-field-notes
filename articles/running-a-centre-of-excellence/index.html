Running a Centre of Excellence for Fabric

The small team that makes everyone else faster: what a Fabric Centre of Excellence owns, which of Microsoft's four structures fits, how to staff and fund it and how to tell whether it is working. Includes the trap that turns a COE into the bottleneck it was built to remove.

11 Oct 2026 Fabric as of October 2026 Checked against Microsoft Learn Level 4 Ankur Napa GovernanceCapacity

Short answer

A Centre of Excellence (COE) is the internal team that sets the standards, trains the builders, watches the platform and takes the escalations, so the business teams can build safely on their own. Microsoft's Fabric adoption roadmap lists its usual duties: mentoring, office hours, co-development and best practice reviews, training, documentation and templates, governance guidance, system oversight and administration, escalated support and communication with the community.

Learn describes four structures: centralised, unified, federated and decentralised. Its own advice is that most organisations do best with unified or federated, which bridge the line between a central team and the business. In practice, start small: three to five people, a champion in each business area and a written charter. That sizing is house practice, not a Learn figure.

The test of a COE is not how much it builds. It is how much the rest of the organisation can build without it.

The decision

Learn's four structures, with the trade-offs it lists:

StructureWhat it isStrengthWeakness
CentralisedOne shared services teamOne point of accountability; easiest to startTends to one-size-fits-all decisions and IT skills over business skills
UnifiedCentral team plus embedded members dedicated to business unitsDeeper understanding of each business area; still one teamEmbedded members report one way and serve another
FederatedCore team plus satellite members inside each business unitBalance of central and local; suits distributed data ownershipNeeds strong leadership, clear expectations and handles competing priorities poorly
DecentralisedEach business unit runs its ownFast and tailoredSilos, inconsistent policy, hard to scale and costly to realign later

Funding is the second decision and it shapes behaviour more than the org chart does.

Funding modelHow it worksWhat it encourages
Cost centreAnnual budget, the COE pushes services outStandards, training, platform health
Profit centreBusiness units fund projects, the COE is pulled inDelivery, but the loudest budget wins
Mixed, with chargebackCore budget plus capacity chargeback to business unitsShared ownership of capacity cost

Most Fabric estates land on federated with mixed funding: a core COE paid centrally, champions in each domain and capacity cost charged back by business unit.

Reference design

Harbour Goods runs Fabric for three business areas. Its COE starts with four people and grows through champions, not headcount.

The standards pack the COE publishes and keeps current:

  • Workspace naming and the rule for when a new workspace is justified
  • Production workspaces connected to Git, promoted through a pipeline
  • Gold layer as a star schema, Direct Lake by default, measures in one place
  • Certification criteria, written down, with a link set in the certification settings
  • A tenant settings register: every non-default setting, who asked and why
  • Training paths: PL-300 for report authors, DP-600 for analytics engineers, DP-700 for data engineers

To measure it, use the adoption roadmap's maturity levels for the COE itself, from 100 (Initial) to 500 (Efficient), scored per area rather than as one number. Pair the score with hard figures the sponsor understands: legacy cost retired, capacity cost per data product, certified items, active creators, time from request to production and the age of open support tickets. That figure list is house practice.

Questions to ask the client

  • Is there a COE today, or a central team quietly doing the job? Who is in it?
  • Who is the executive sponsor? Does their authority cover both IT and the business units?
  • Do business users see the central team as help or as a gate?
  • What will the COE not do? Write that down first.
  • Is it funded as a cost centre, by projects or by chargeback? Who pays for capacity?
  • Are there people in the business already building good things on their own? They are the first champions.
  • How will you know in six months that it worked? Name the figures now.
  • Which certifications does the team hold? Who is paying for the time to get them?

Review checklist

  • A one-page charter names scope, services, structure and what is out of scope.
  • The sponsor is named and meets the COE lead at least quarterly.
  • Every role in the table has a person. No one person holds all the platform admin rights.
  • Office hours run on a fixed slot and someone records the recurring questions.
  • Certification criteria are written and a reviewer is named per domain.
  • Capacity is reviewed monthly and the top consumers are told, with fixes.
  • Tenant setting changes are logged with a reason.
  • Each domain has at least one recognised champion.
  • Maturity is scored per area every quarter, alongside the hard figures.
  • Funding and chargeback rules are agreed with finance, not assumed.

Anti-patterns

  • The COE builds everything. Every request lands in one queue and the business waits. It becomes the bottleneck it was meant to remove. Co-develop instead, then hand over.
  • The COE as police. Rules without office hours or templates teach people to build around you, usually in a personal workspace.
  • Counting reports and workspaces. That rewards sprawl. Count cost retired, certified items and time to production.
  • A COE made only of IT. Learn warns that centralised teams drift towards IT skills over business skills. Put business analysts in it.
  • Champions with no recognition. Unpaid, unthanked champions stop answering questions by the second quarter.
  • No funding model. If nobody decided who pays for capacity, the first expensive notebook decides it for you.

On the brewhouse floor

A good brewery quality lab works the way a good COE should. The lab does not brew every batch. It writes the sampling method, checks the pH meter against buffer solutions before the morning shift, trains the brewers to pull a clean sample and steps in when a result looks wrong. The brewers do the brewing. When the lab starts taking every sample itself, the queue at the lab door becomes the slowest step in the brewhouse, which is exactly what happens to a COE that builds every report.
