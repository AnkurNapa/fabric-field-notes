Data analysis basics, lesson 7 of 8

The statistics that actually matter

Lesson 7 of Data analysis basics. Most business questions need four ideas, not a statistics degree: mean against median, the count behind every average, ratios of totals and honest percentage change. Use them on the clean Harbour Goods orders and watch the store with the best average turn out to have four sales.

Short answer

An average on its own can mislead. Report the median next to the mean, say how many rows sit behind each number, calculate rates from totals rather than averaging them and give percentage changes with the raw numbers beside them. Those four habits cover most of what a junior analyst needs. None of them needs more than the SQL you already know.

Why it matters for your career

Managers act on averages. If the average hides one huge order or rests on four sales, the decision built on it is wrong and your name is on the slide. Knowing when an average lies is what makes your numbers trusted.

The idea

The mean is what most people call the average: add everything up and divide by the count. The median is the middle value once you sort them, so half the rows are above it and half below. One very large value drags the mean up but barely moves the median.

On the 56 clean Harbour Goods order lines from lesson 6:

MeasureOrder line value (INR)
Mean2,748.57
Median2,224.05
Smallest549.00
Largest8,796.00

The mean is about 500 INR above the median because a handful of big lines, such as four Bluetooth speakers for 8,796 INR, pull it up. A "typical" order line is nearer the median.

Three more ideas:

  • The count behind the number. An average of four rows can swing wildly when one more sale arrives. Always show the count next to it.
  • Ratio of totals, not average of ratios. The average of the discount column treats a 549 INR towel and an 8,796 INR speaker order as equal. The share of money actually given away is total discount divided by total list price.
  • Percentage change. New minus old, divided by old. Always give the two raw numbers as well, so nobody has to trust the percentage alone.

Try it in Fabric

Open lh_harbour, switch to the SQL analytics endpoint and select New SQL query. Every query uses the dbo.orders_clean view from lesson 6.

  1. Mean and median per store. PERCENTILE_CONT(0.5) finds the median. In T-SQL it is written with an OVER clause, so the query uses DISTINCT to keep one row per store:
SELECT DISTINCT
    store_id,
    COUNT(*) OVER (PARTITION BY store_id) AS order_lines,
    CAST(AVG(revenue_inr) OVER (PARTITION BY store_id) AS decimal(12,2)) AS mean_line,
    PERCENTILE_CONT(0.5) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY revenue_inr)
        OVER (PARTITION BY store_id) AS median_line
FROM dbo.orders_clean
ORDER BY mean_line DESC;
  1. The discount two ways:
SELECT
    CAST(AVG(discount) AS decimal(5,4)) AS average_of_discounts,
    CAST(1 - SUM(revenue_inr) / SUM(quantity * unit_price) AS decimal(5,4)) AS share_given_away
FROM dbo.orders_clean;
  1. Monthly revenue, for the percentage change:
SELECT
    LEFT(CAST(order_date AS varchar(10)), 7) AS order_month,
    COUNT(*) AS order_lines,
    SUM(revenue_inr) AS revenue_inr
FROM dbo.orders_clean
GROUP BY LEFT(CAST(order_date AS varchar(10)), 7)
ORDER BY order_month;

Step 2 returns 0.0330 and 0.0428. The plain average says Harbour Goods gives away 3.3 per cent; the money says 4.3 per cent, because the bigger orders got the bigger discounts.

Step 3 returns July 22 lines and 68134.50, August 17 and 45059.60, September 17 and 40725.75. August is down 33.9 per cent on July and September is down 9.6 per cent on August.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Reporting the mean alone. Give the median too. If they differ a lot, say why.
  • Ranking on tiny counts. A store with four sales should not top a league table without a note.
  • Averaging percentages. Go back to the totals and divide once.
  • Calling three months a trend. Two falls in a row is a reason to look closer, not a forecast.
  • Percentages without numbers. "Up 50 per cent" from 2 to 3 sales means nothing. Show both.

On the brewhouse floor

A winery compares the sugar level of fruit from each vineyard block at harvest. One block shows the highest average, but it sent only two loads, one of them from the sunniest corner. The median per block and the count of loads tell a calmer story. The same goes for juice yield: litres per tonne must be total litres divided by total tonnes, not the average of each load's yield. Otherwise the small loads count as much as the big ones.
