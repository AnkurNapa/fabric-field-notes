Choosing the right visual

Pick the visual from the question, not from the gallery. A short table of question types and the Power BI visual that answers each, then four visuals on the Harbour Goods report with the numbers they should show.

11 Oct 2026 Fabric as of October 2026 Checked against Microsoft Learn Ankur Napa Power BI

Short answer

Every chart answers one kind of question. Start from the question, name its kind and the visual follows. A single number goes on a card. Change over time goes on a line or column chart. Comparing categories goes on a bar chart, sorted. Exact values people will read go in a table or matrix. Pie charts, gauges and maps are for narrow cases and rarely the right first choice.

Why it matters for your career

Reviewers judge a report in about five seconds. If the reader has to work out what a chart is saying, they stop trusting the page. Choosing the plain, correct visual is a skill hiring managers notice because so few portfolios show it.

The idea

Question kindExample on Harbour GoodsVisualAvoid
One number nowWhat are net sales this quarter?CardA gauge with an invented maximum
Change over timeAre sales rising month by month?Column chart (few periods) or line chart (many)Pie per month
Compare categoriesWhich city sells most?Bar chart, sorted largest firstUnsorted columns, 3D anything
Part of a wholeWhat share is Electronics?Stacked bar or a 100% stacked barPie with more than three slices
Two measures togetherSales and margin by categoryMatrix, or a combo chartTwo pies side by side
Exact lookupMargin by category and monthMatrix with conditional formattingA chart with every data label on

Why bars and not pies? People compare lengths along a common baseline far more accurately than angles. Six cities in a pie are six slices of nearly the same size. Six cities as sorted bars are a ranking you read in one glance.

Bar or column? Microsoft's guidance is short: column charts for time, bar charts when category names are long. City names fit either; product names such as "Non-stick frying pan 24 cm" want bars.

Line or column for time? A line implies continuity between points. With three months, columns are honest; with 52 weeks, a line shows the shape better.

Try it in Fabric

Open rpt_harbour_sales and select Edit.

  1. Card. Select the Card visual in the Visualizations pane and add Net sales, Gross margin % and Orders. The current card visual can show several values in one visual, which keeps the row tidy.
  2. Column chart. Add a Clustered column chart with orders[order_month] on the X-axis and Net sales on the Y-axis. Sort by order_month ascending (the visual's ... menu > Sort axis).
  3. Bar chart. Add a Clustered bar chart with stores[city] on the Y-axis and Net sales on the X-axis. Sort by Net sales descending.
  4. Matrix. Add a Matrix with products[category] on Rows, orders[order_month] on Columns and Gross margin % on Values.

Check it worked:

  • Columns: July 168,660, August 154,160, September 183,991.
  • Bars: Kolkata on top at 112,569 and Mumbai at the bottom at 57,693.
  • Matrix: Electronics is the weak row, with margin falling from 33.8 per cent in July to 29.6 per cent in September. Home and Kitchen stay between 37 and 40 per cent.

Now try the wrong visual on purpose. Change the bar chart to a pie chart. Kolkata, Bengaluru, Delhi and Chennai are within 25,000 of each other and the slices look identical. Change it back.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Choosing by looks. A donut is not more modern than a bar. It is harder to read.
  • Unsorted bars. Alphabetical order hides the ranking, which is the whole point of the chart.
  • Too many series. A line chart with 20 lines is a plate of noodles. Show the top five and group the rest as "Other", or use small multiples.
  • Gauges with made-up maximums. A gauge needs a real target. Without one, the needle position means nothing.
  • Dual axes by default. Two Y-axes with different scales make any two lines look related. Use them only when the relationship is the point.

On the brewhouse floor

At Winery A someone once put the sugar readings of twelve fermenting tanks on one pie chart. A pie of sugar levels answers no question anyone in the cellar asks. The question is "which tanks are still fermenting and which have stopped too early?" That is a line per tank over days, falling towards dry, with the one line that flattens high standing out at once. Same data, right visual. The stuck ferment gets caught on day four instead of day nine.
