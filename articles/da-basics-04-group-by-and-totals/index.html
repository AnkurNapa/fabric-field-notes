Data analysis basics, lesson 4 of 8

Grouping and totals

Lesson 4 of Data analysis basics. GROUP BY turns many rows into one row per group, aggregates like SUM and COUNT do the arithmetic and HAVING filters the groups. Build revenue per store and per month for Harbour Goods.

11 Oct 2026 Fabric as of October 2026 Checked against Microsoft Learn Ankur Napa LakehouseWarehouse

Short answer

GROUP BY collapses many rows into one row per group, such as one row per store. Aggregate functions do the arithmetic for each group: COUNT counts rows, SUM adds, AVG averages, MIN and MAX find the extremes. WHERE filters rows before grouping; HAVING filters the groups after. Those four ideas answer most "how much, per what" questions a manager will ever ask.

Why it matters for your career

Almost every report is a grouped total underneath. If you can write the grouping yourself, you can check any dashboard number in a minute instead of trusting it.

The idea

Picture sorting the till receipts into piles, one per store, then writing a total on each pile. That is GROUP BY store_id plus SUM.

The rule to remember: every column in SELECT must either be in GROUP BY or be inside an aggregate. Otherwise SQL cannot know which of a store's 15 dates to show you, so it refuses.

The order SQL works in is not the order you write it:

StepClauseWhat happens
1FROMread the table
2WHEREdrop rows you do not want
3GROUP BYmake the piles
4HAVINGdrop piles you do not want
5SELECTcalculate the columns
6ORDER BYsort the result

This is why you cannot use a SELECT alias inside WHERE: it has not been calculated yet. ORDER BY comes last, so there you can.

Try it in Fabric

Use the orders table in lh_harbour from lesson 1.

  1. Open lh_harbour, switch the ribbon dropdown to SQL analytics endpoint and select New SQL query.
  2. Revenue per store, keeping only stores with at least ten order lines:
SELECT
    store_id,
    COUNT(*)      AS order_lines,
    SUM(quantity) AS units,
    CAST(SUM(quantity * unit_price * (1 - discount)) AS decimal(12,2)) AS revenue_inr
FROM dbo.orders
GROUP BY store_id
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 10
ORDER BY revenue_inr DESC;
  1. Revenue per month. LEFT(..., 7) keeps the first seven characters of the date, such as 2026-07:
SELECT
    LEFT(CAST(order_date AS varchar(10)), 7) AS order_month,
    COUNT(*) AS order_lines,
    CAST(SUM(quantity * unit_price * (1 - discount)) AS decimal(12,2)) AS revenue_inr
FROM dbo.orders
GROUP BY LEFT(CAST(order_date AS varchar(10)), 7)
ORDER BY order_month;
  1. Finally, compare two counts: SELECT COUNT(*) AS all_rows, COUNT(quantity) AS rows_with_quantity FROM dbo.orders;

Check it worked. Step 2 returns three stores:

store_idorder_linesunitsrevenue_inr

Remove the HAVING line and you get seven rows: ST-007, ST-011 and ST-022 come back, plus the mystery ST-099 with 854.05. Step 3 returns 2026-07 with 23 lines and 68683.50, 2026-08 with 19 lines and 43961.60 and 2026-09 with 18 lines and 43624.75. All months together come to 156269.85 INR.

Step 4 returns 60 and 59. COUNT(*) counts rows; COUNT(quantity) counts only rows where quantity is not NULL. The same rule applies to SUM and AVG: they skip NULLs without telling you. Hold on to that 156269.85. In lesson 6 you will see it is 2,350 INR too high.

Common beginner mistakes

  • A column missing from GROUP BY. The error says the column "is invalid in the select list". Add it to GROUP BY or wrap it in an aggregate.
  • Using WHERE for a group condition. WHERE COUNT(*) >= 10 fails because counts do not exist until after grouping. Use HAVING.
  • Averaging an average. The average of three monthly averages is not the quarter's average unless every month had the same number of rows. Go back to the rows.
  • Trusting SUM over NULLs. A blank quantity silently drops out of the total. Count the NULLs before you report the sum.

On the brewhouse floor

A brewery's packaging log has one row per pallet: date, beer, line and cases. GROUP BY beer with SUM(cases) gives the week's output per brand. HAVING SUM(cases) < 200 flags the brands that need a second run before the weekend. The trap is the same: a pallet with a blank case count drops out of the sum. The shortfall lands on the brewer who did not cause it.
