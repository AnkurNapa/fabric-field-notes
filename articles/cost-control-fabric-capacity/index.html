Cost control: size, reserve, pause and charge back with real numbers

A Fabric bill of USD 7,008 a month cut to USD 3,610.67 without touching a single report. Measure CU use by item, check whether the capacity can shrink, reserve what runs all day, pause what does not and split the bill by domain. Prices are Central India list prices checked on 11 October 2026.

Ankur Napa 11 Oct 2026, 9 min read

Short answer

Most Fabric bills are too high for boring reasons: a production capacity on pay-as-you-go that never stops, a dev capacity running through the night for nobody and one item eating a sixth of the capacity by reloading everything every run. Fix them in that order. Check whether the capacity can shrink, reserve the CUs that run around the clock, pause the ones that do not, then send each team its share. On the Harbour Goods example below that takes the monthly bill from USD 7,008.00 to USD 3,610.67, a 48.5 per cent cut, with no change to any report.

Prices used here

All prices are USD list prices for Central India, pulled from the Azure Retail Prices API (service Microsoft Fabric) on 11 October 2026. They are before any EA, MCA or CSP discount. Microsoft bills in USD and converts at its published rates, so an INR invoice moves with the exchange rate.

The 1-year reservation works out at 40.5 per cent below pay-as-you-go, the same discount the Microsoft pricing page quotes. At list price the 3-year term adds no further discount, so do not sign one without checking what your agreement actually says. Monthly figures below use 730 hours.

The symptom

Harbour Goods runs two F SKU capacities in Central India, both on pay-as-you-go:

  • caphgprodf32, an F32 for production, running all month: 32 x 0.20 x 730 = USD 4,672.00.
  • caphgdevf16, an F16 for dev and test, also running all month: 16 x 0.20 x 730 = USD 2,336.00.

USD 7,008.00 a month. Finance wants it lower and wants to know which team is spending it. The platform team's first instinct is to drop production to an F16. Measure before you do that.

Measure it

1. CU use by item. A capacity admin installs the Microsoft Fabric Chargeback app from AppSource. Install it into a Pro workspace, not one on the capacity it reports on, or the report adds to the load it is measuring. The app breaks CU use down by workspace, item, domain and user. The sample cost-control.csv is a synthetic 40-row export of it for September 2026: one row per item on the production capacity, with its CU hours.

import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("/lakehouse/default/Files/cost-control.csv")
total = df["cu_hours"].sum()
print(total) # 14404
print(round(total / (32 * 24 * 30) * 100, 1)) # 62.5 (% of September's F32 CU hours)
print(df.nlargest(3, "cu_hours")[["item", "item_type", "cu_hours"]])

The F32 had 23,040 CU hours in September and used 14,404, an average of 62.5 per cent. Averages flatter. Open the Fabric Capacity Metrics app as well and look at the Utilization chart for peaks, because throttling follows the smoothed peak, not the monthly mean.

2. The top items. Three items carry 34.8 per cent of the month:

The first one is the story. It reloads the whole deliveries table every two hours to pick up a few hundred new rows.

3. Can production shrink? An F16 has 16 x 720 = 11,520 CU hours in a 30-day month. September used 14,404. Even if the dataflow vanished entirely, 12,052 CU hours is 104.6 per cent of an F16 before any peak. The downsize idea dies here, which is the point of measuring first. Downsizing a busy capacity with capacity overage left on would have been worse: overage bills at USD 0.60 per CU hour.

4. Who runs when. In the Azure portal open Cost Management > Cost analysis, filter to the two capacities and group by resource. The dev capacity bills 730 hours a month. The dev team works roughly 08:00 to 20:00 on weekdays.

Fix it

Step 1: reserve production

Production runs all day every day, so reserve it. In the Azure portal go to Reservations > Add, choose Microsoft Fabric, pick the Central India region, a 1-year term and a quantity of 32 CUs. Scope it to the subscription that holds the capacity.

A reservation is a billing discount, not a resource. Nothing changes on the capacity. It bills 32 CUs at USD 86.83 each instead of USD 146: 32 x 1,042 / 12 = USD 2,778.67 a month. Two details matter. Reserve only the CUs you run 24x7, because scaling below the reserved quantity does not shrink the bill. And reserve in the capacity's own region; a Central India reservation does nothing for a South India capacity.

Step 2: pause dev outside working hours

Reservations pay off only above about 59.5 per cent uptime (USD 1,042 against 8,760 hours at USD 0.20). The dev capacity at 60 hours a week runs 260 hours a month, 35.6 per cent, so keep it on pay-as-you-go and pause it.

Give an automation account a custom Azure role with only Microsoft.Fabric/capacities/read, write, suspend/action and resume/action on the capacity, then schedule two runbooks or pipeline steps:

# 20:00 on weekdays and all weekend
az fabric capacity suspend --resource-group rg-hg-fabric --capacity-name caphgdevf16

# 08:00 on weekdays
az fabric capacity resume --resource-group rg-hg-fabric --capacity-name caphgdevf16

New cost: 16 x 0.20 x 260 = USD 832.00 a month.

Step 3: fix the top item

Rebuild df_deliveries_full_reload to load only new and changed deliveries, using incremental copy or a notebook with a watermark on dispatched_at. This does not reduce the reserved bill, which is fixed. What it buys is headroom: if the item drops to a tenth of its September use, production falls from 62.5 to 53.3 per cent average utilisation. That is next year's growth absorbed without moving to an F64.

Step 4: charge back by domain

Assign every workspace to a domain first. Anything without one lands in No domain in the Chargeback app. Nobody pays for No domain. Then split the reserved production cost by CU share:

reserved_month = round(32 * 1042 / 12, 2) # 2778.67
by_domain = (df.groupby("domain")["cu_hours"].sum()
               .sort_values(ascending=False)
               .to_frame("cu_hours"))
by_domain["share_pct"] = (by_domain["cu_hours"] / total * 100).round(1)
by_domain["usd"] = (by_domain["cu_hours"] / total * reserved_month).round(2)
print(by_domain)

Check the result in Cost analysis at the end of the first full month. The production line should show the reservation and nothing on the pay-as-you-go meter unless you scaled above 32 CUs.

Where this breaks

  • Pausing a reserved capacity saves nothing. The reservation bills whether the capacity runs or not. Pause only pay-as-you-go capacities.
  • Pausing bills the carry-forward at once. When a capacity pauses, smoothed future usage and overages are added to the bill immediately. A heavy evening job followed by a 20:00 pause shows as a spike, not a saving. Schedule big dev jobs earlier.
  • A paused capacity breaks things quietly. Scheduled refreshes and pipelines on that capacity fail while it is paused. Move anything that must run overnight to production.
  • Reserving the peak. If production sometimes needs 64 CUs on Monday mornings, reserve 32 and scale up on pay-as-you-go for those hours. Microsoft's own planning guide notes that extra reserved capacity only pays off if you need it more than about four days a week.
  • Spark on-demand billing sits outside all of this. If you move Spark off the capacity to on-demand billing, it bills at the pay-as-you-go rate with no reservation discount.

On the brewhouse floor

A distillery splits the same way. Distillery A runs one production capacity shared by the stillhouse dashboards, the warehouse team tracking casks and finance closing the month. Reserve it, then charge each team by CU share exactly like the table above. The detail that matters on that floor is the dev capacity: the data team that builds the maturation reports also works the cask audit week, when nobody opens a notebook for five days. Pausing that capacity for the week is worth more than any query tuning.
